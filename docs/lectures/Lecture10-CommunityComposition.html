The organizing question

Single Large or Several Small: SLOSS

Equal area gives us a comparison. There is still huge debate about the right strategy.

A long-running conservation debate: Simberloff & Abele (1976).

Ask for a show of hands before introducing any metric. What assumptions motivated each choice? Larger continuous habitat may support processes and populations that small patches cannot; separated reserves may capture different environments and species. These are hypotheses to evaluate, rather than universal rules. Simberloff and Abele challenged the inference that one largest possible refuge must always be best. This lecture does not settle the broader fragmentation debate. The three circle areas sum exactly to the large circle area; distances and habitat quality in this schematic have no quantitative meaning.
Define the conservation objective

“More biodiversity” can mean several things

If the goal is…We need to examine…
Conserve more speciesSpecies richness
Avoid dominance by a few speciesAbundance distributions (Entropy and evenness)
Capture different communitiesBeta diversity (turnover)
Protect rare speciesEndemism and range information

Identify the conservation goal before choosing the focal biodiversity index.

These objectives overlap but can rank sites differently. Species identity matters as well: a network can be species rich while missing a focal threatened species. High Shannon diversity is not inherently the goal of every conservation program. A naturally dominated community can still be valuable. Today we will make each measure concrete and use differences among measures to ask better biological questions. Demographic persistence, threat reduction and habitat quality require information beyond these indices.

Species richness (S)

The most basic measure of community diversity

Richness is simple to interpret, but obviously communities with the same richness can be very different in other meaningful ways.

Each animal icon represents one occurrence record; letters and colors distinguish the invented species. Count the different species, rather than the icons. Additional A records would not increase observed richness, because A is already recorded. One C record in Park 1 would increase its richness to three. Richness ignores how records are divided among recorded species and ignores identities when reduced to a scalar. Two parks can each have richness three while containing entirely different species. Richness can also increase with sampling: more records often discover additional species. We will return to that problem before choosing a reserve strategy.
Entropy / Shannon diversity (H')

How similar are species in their abundances?

How uncertain would you be about the species of a randomly chosen record?

In the even community, all three species are equally plausible on a random draw. In the dominated community, guessing the dominant species is usually correct. Shannon entropy quantifies that uncertainty. Both richness and balance influence it. In standardized counts of individuals, proportions can represent relative abundance; in this lab they represent relative frequencies of occurrence records. A conspicuous, accessible or heavily reported species can dominate records without dominating the animal community. These values use natural logarithms.

Shannon diversity is not comparable across sites with different richness. Scaling H' by S gives evenness, which is directly comparable.

The dominated community has the same Shannon diversity as about 1.34 equally frequent species.

The effective count is a comparison with an idealized equally frequent community, not a claim that fractional species were observed. Dividing by richness gives one when all recorded species are equally frequent and a lower value under dominance. This is exp(H)/S, rather than the also-common Pielou measure H/log(S). Keep the name tied to the formula students actually use. Richness is still three in both rows. Empty record sets do not supply an estimable evenness value. Source: Jost explains why transforming entropy into effective numbers gives a more intuitive diversity scale.
Quantifying diversity at different scales; Alpha, Beta, and Gamma Diversity

We want to measure diversity in ways that capture local AND global patterns, and differences among local sites.

Alpha (α) = local richness. Gamma (γ) = global richness. Beta (β; Average turnover) = γ / mean α.

Larger β values indicate more unique local communities.

Walk through the repeated network first. Adding more copies of ABC does not add species to the pooled list. In the complementary network, A is in all parks while B through G are each in one. The sum of park richnesses is nine in both networks, but gamma is three versus seven. Multiplicative beta is one versus seven thirds. These definitions use richness, with parks weighted equally when averaging alpha. Beta diversity is a family of ideas and measures; state the definition rather than treating every beta index as numerically interchangeable.
Pairwise beta-diversity (presence/absence)

Jaccard Dissimilarity: How much do species lists differ between sites?

Dissimilarity = 1 − shared / union. Identical lists = 0. No shared species = 1.

Use presence/absence with scipy.spatial.distance.jaccard.

Students can count the shared and union species directly from the figure. A species absent from both lists is excluded from the union and does not make the parks more similar. A rare species recorded once contributes to this presence/absence comparison just as much as a commonly recorded species. Keep the same species-column order for both vectors. SciPy returns dissimilarity rather than similarity. If both parks have no records, a software convention of zero distance supplies no biological evidence of identical communities.
Pairwise beta-diversity (abundance)

Bray–Curtis Dissimilarity accounts for differences in abundance

Bray–Curtis = Σ |ai − bi| / Σ (ai + bi)

Nonnegative counts; at least one record overall. scipy.spatial.distance.braycurtis.

Both parks contain the same three species, so Jaccard is zero. Species A differs by seven records, B by seven, and C by zero. Dividing fourteen by the twenty total records gives 0.7. For nonnegative vectors with positive combined total, Bray–Curtis lies between zero and one. Zero means identical count vectors; one means no species with positive counts shared. A pair of all-zero vectors has an undefined denominator. The species colors match the icon legend. These invented records illustrate count differences, not inferred animal abundance.
Differential sampling affects observed richness

Sample-based rarefaction: Resample to the same number of occurrence data points

Sites with more occurrences may be more species rich because of differential sampling effort. Rarefaction is one way to evaluate sensitivity to this.

These are exact expected richness curves under uniform record draws without replacement, not fitted lab results. At 100 records, A contains its whole sample and therefore all four species. Each singleton in B has a one-half chance of being drawn, while the dominant species is certain to occur, giving one plus five halves, or 3.5. At all 200 records, B has six species. The example shows that dominance and sample size both matter. The lab instead approximates common-size comparisons by repeated draws. Its quantiles summarize draws rather than confidence intervals for true richness. Repeated occurrences need not be independent individuals, so call this record-based rarefaction.
A different conservation objective

Endemism means geographic restriction

Full geographic range

Is a species restricted to Maine, an island, or another named region?

Requires evidence about its distribution beyond the focal parks.

Restriction in our sample

In how many of these four parks has a species been recorded?

A useful proxy, with a much narrower interpretation.

“Recorded only in Park 2” does not establish “endemic to Park 2.”

Reference extent matters: Daru et al. (2020), this week's discussion paper.

A species can be endemic to a broad region while common within that region. A species found in only one sampled park can also be widespread outside the park network. Daru and colleagues examined how endemism patterns depend on the scope and definition of the analysis. For today's lab, focus on explicitly naming the reference set; spatial aggregation and moving-window analyses belong to the later lab. We have occurrence frequencies in four unequal-area parks, not complete geographic range sizes. Use sample-exclusive and endemism proxy when interpreting these results.
Weighted endemism proxy

Give more weight to species in fewer parks

For a park: WE = sum of 1/r for its species; r = number of parks recording that species.

Toy park occupancy, not full range size. Range-weighted approach: Crisp et al. (2001).

Keep the reference set of four parks fixed. Every species in a park contributes something, but a one-park species contributes four times as much as a four-park species. A's abundance of eight in each park does not enter this weight. WE is a sum, so it can increase by adding species even if those species are widespread. Published geographic WE commonly weights by occupied geographic units across a broader range. Our park-based version is a proxy; units differ in area and the available occurrences do not constitute complete range maps. The formula teaches the inverse-occupancy logic without claiming calibrated regional endemism.

CWE: mean restriction weight of the species recorded.

Would adding a widespread species necessarily improve CWE?

No. Adding a species with a weight below the current average increases WE while decreasing CWE. Park 2 has the larger sum, but Park 3 has a larger average restriction weight. Neither is a universally superior conservation score. CWE is not an independent estimate of range rarity and does not automatically remove sampling bias. The worked values use the same matrix as the preceding slides. Crisp et al. 2001 introduced the correction to help distinguish endemism patterns from species richness; keep that distinction tied to the conservation objective.
Keep the reference explicit

New records outside the network change the weight

The focal park did not lose C. Our knowledge of C's distribution changed.

Hypothetical additional surveys. Endemism depends on the stated reference: Daru et al. (2020).

The left view has four surveyed parks; the right adds three parks, all recording C. Its observed occupancy therefore increases from one to four. The denominator is occupied parks, not the total number of parks: its new weight is one quarter, not one seventh. A newly surveyed park with no C record would not by itself change C's inverse occupancy. This is an illustration of changing information and reference extent. It does not establish a full geographic range. Keep the reference fixed when comparing WE or CWE values within an analysis.

Lab definition: record-rare = at most five records across all four parks.

Reporting rarity is not population rarity, geographic restriction, or threat status.

C and D are both sample-exclusive, but only C is record-rare. E also meets both conditions. A hypothetical species with one record in every park would be record-rare while having the maximum park occupancy, demonstrating the opposite combination. The threshold of five is a teaching choice, not a biological boundary or conservation designation. A low count might reflect low abundance, poor detectability, little reporting or the simulation's allocation. Some invented species have only a few points by design. Ask students how changing the threshold to ten would affect the species counted and whether that should change their conservation recommendation.

Explain disagreements among metrics; they often reveal different biological priorities.

Avoid combining all indices into an unexplained score. A strong recommendation states its objective, identifies species gained or missed, reports numerical evidence and explains uncertainty. If one strategy has greater gamma while another has higher CWE, that is not a mathematical contradiction. It is a choice between total representation and average restriction under the chosen proxy. A focal-species protection objective could justify a different choice again. The records cannot tell us which outcome the community or society should value most.
Representation is one part of conservation

Presence today does not guarantee persistence

  • Does each park contain breeding and nonbreeding habitat?
  • Can populations recruit, survive and move among suitable sites?
  • How do disturbance, disease, land use and future climate affect them?

A diversity table supports a reserve comparison. A real decision also needs habitat and demographic evidence.

Life-cycle habitat motivation: Semlitsch & Bodie (2003).

Return to the pond and forest example. Occurrences within a proposed boundary do not establish protection of every habitat needed to complete a life cycle, a viable population, or functional connectivity. These are candidate questions for field research, not effects estimated by the exercise. Students should be able to name a concrete next measurement, such as repeated breeding surveys, survival or recruitment estimates, habitat condition, or movement across roads. The imaginary polygons and empirical-template simulation intentionally isolate a tractable representation exercise.

A related conservation question

What if we hold park number equal and change shape?

Compare representation first; test proposed shape mechanisms with additional evidence.

Shape illustration, not the assignment's actual locations. Two parks per design, 500 km² each.

This motivates the blob-versus-stripe assignment, which reuses the metric sequence. Number and area can be equal while locations, orientations and environmental coverage differ. Long narrow parks may intersect different habitats, while compact parks have different edge-to-area geometry; neither statement predicts a universal winner. A one-off geographic comparison cannot isolate a causal effect of shape. Repeated placements, matched habitat and explicit models or measurements of the proposed mechanism would strengthen inference. The assignment's sampler has geographic range templates but no explicit edge, movement or survival processes.

What would change your mind?

One network captures more species. The other captures fewer species, but includes the only recorded population of a focal species.

  • What is your conservation objective?
  • Which metric helps, and what does it leave out?
  • What observation would most strengthen your decision?
Give students a minute to discuss in pairs. Do not supply a universal correct reserve choice. Listen for a stated objective, a distinction between observed records and actual populations, and a practical evidence request. If students propose an endemic species, ask how its full range was determined. If they propose greater richness, ask whether sampling was comparable and which species are added.
